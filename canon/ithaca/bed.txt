She comes down. She sits opposite you by the fire. She does not
say anything for a very long time.

Telemachus loses his temper about it. He says: mother, what is
wrong with you, no other woman would sit apart from her husband
like this, your heart is a stone.

She says, without looking away from you: if he is Odysseus, we
have signs between us. Ours. Nobody else knows them.

Then she says it to the nurse, over your head, in the flattest
voice anybody uses in this whole poem:

Eurycleia. Move the bed out of the bedroom. Make it up for him
outside the chamber he built himself.

And you are on your feet before you have decided to be.

You say: woman, who has moved my bed? Nobody could. Not without
cutting.

There was an olive tree growing inside the walls when you built
that room, thick as a pillar, in full leaf. You built the room
around the tree. You cut the trunk off above the roots, squared
it, drilled it, made it the corner post of the bed frame,
inlaid the whole thing with gold, silver, ivory. The bed has one
leg that is a living root going down into the ground under the
house.

That is the sign. Not a scar, not a dog, not a bow. A piece of
carpentry only two people on earth have ever seen.

What comes back is what is different between two people's
memories of the same object after twenty years.

Then look at her face while she works out that the bed has not
been moved, that nobody has cut the post, that the man by the
fire is the man who cut the tree.

Homer's picture: land showing up to men swimming, whose ship the
sea has broken up in the swell, the few of them who get out of
the grey water with the salt caked on them, climbing onto a
beach, glad, out of it.

He gives that comparison to her, about you.

She holds onto your neck. She says: do not be angry with me, you
were always the sharpest of men, the gods gave us grief because
they could not bear us being young together.

Athena holds the night back. She keeps Dawn's horses in their
stalls at the edge of the world for as long as it takes.
