You walk down to the house the next morning. Eumaeus goes ahead.
You come behind on a stick, in the rags, taking your time,
because that is what a man of seventy does.

At the gate there is a dog lying on a heap of dung, in the sun,
covered in ticks.

You bred him. You picked him out of the litter yourself before
you sailed. You never worked him -- you left before he was old
enough. The young men in the house hunted with him for a while
after you went, then they stopped bothering.

His name is Argos.

He is the oldest thing on this island that remembers you.

    ls -l ithaca/hall

The long listing has a column you have never had a reason to
read on any island of this voyage: the date. Every file carries
the moment it was last written to. Everything in that directory
was put there the morning your voyage was dealt out, so the
dates are all the same, all recent, all meaningless.

Except one.

    ls -lt ithaca/hall

The -t sorts by that date instead of by name, newest first, so
the oldest thing falls to the bottom of the list.

    ls -lt ithaca/hall | tail -n 1

There he is. Twenty years old, in a directory where nothing else
is more than a few days old, because nothing has been done to
him since before you left.

    less ithaca/hall/argos.dog

He lifts his head. He gets his ears up. He is too weak to stand,
so he does the only thing left: he moves his tail, twice.

He does not bark. He knows better than to bark. He knows exactly
who this is, off a smell he has not had in twenty years, from a
man his own household cannot pick out of a crowd.

You look away. You wipe your face with the back of your hand
where Eumaeus cannot see it.

Then Argos dies. That hour, in the sun, having seen you.

Nobody else in this house will know you for another four days.
The dog took eight seconds.

Then you go in through your own front door for the first time in
twenty years, on a stick, behind a swineherd, to beg for scraps
at your own table.

You spend the evening being insulted by young men who are eating
your cattle. One of them throws a footstool at you. You take it
on the shoulder. You stand there like a rock, thinking about
where each of them is sitting.

Later, when the hall empties, your wife comes down to question
the beggar.
