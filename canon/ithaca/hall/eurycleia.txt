Your wife comes down to the hall in the evening to question the
beggar.

She does it properly: where are you from, have you seen him,
what was he wearing. You lie to her for an hour. You describe
the clasp on the cloak you were wearing when you left -- a hound
holding a fawn down, worked in gold -- because you know she made
it, because you want to see what she does.

She weeps. You watch her weep with your own face arranged into
somebody else's, and Homer says your eyes stayed as steady as
horn or iron in your head, which is either the most impressive
thing in this poem or the coldest.

Then she offers the old man a bath. He refuses. He says: no
young woman is washing my feet. If there is an old servant here
who has been through something, she can.

There is one. Her name is Eurycleia. Your father bought her when
she was a girl for twenty oxen. She nursed you. She put you on
her knees.

She fetches the basin. She looks at the beggar in the firelight
while the water is heating up. She says: I have never seen
anybody so like him. The feet, the hands, the voice.

Then she washes you.

Her hands go up your right leg above the knee.

She drops your foot. The bronze basin goes over, all the water
across the floor, the noise of it filling the hall.

Joy, then terror, both at once. Her eyes fill. She says your
name.

You get your right hand round her throat before she can say it
again. You pull her in with the left. You tell her: nurse, do
you want to destroy me. You fed me at your own breast. Not one
word, to anybody, including her.

She says: you know what I am made of. I am stone. I am iron.

Then she offers, quietly, to tell you which of the household
women have thrown in with the suitors against your wife.

You tell her not to. You say: I will find that out myself.
