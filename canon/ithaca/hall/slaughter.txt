You put the second arrow through Antinous' throat while he is
lifting a two-handled gold cup to drink.

He goes over backwards off the stool. The cup goes one way, the
table the other, the bread into the blood. They are still
shouting at you about it being an accident when you tell them
who you are.

Then the rags come off.

You say: dogs. You thought I was never coming back from Troy.
You ate my house. You gave orders to my people under my own
roof. You courted my wife with me alive. Now the ropes of
destruction are fastened on all of you.

Four against a hundred and eight: you, Telemachus, Eumaeus, the
cowherd Philoetius. Athena in the roof beams in the shape of a
swallow, doing very little, mostly watching.

It takes the rest of the afternoon.

Homer's picture of the hall afterwards: fish that fishermen have
dragged up out of the grey sea in a net onto a hollow of the
beach, lying heaped on the sand, longing for the water, with the
sun taking the life out of them.

When it is done you have Eurycleia bring the twelve women of the
household who took the suitors' side against your wife. They
carry the bodies out. They scrub the tables. Then Telemachus
hangs them in the yard, because you told him to.

Homer's picture for that one: thrushes or doves caught in a
snare in a thicket on their way to roost.

Nobody in this poem says a word against it. Nobody in this poem
says a word for them either. They get four lines, then they get
the comparison about the birds, then it moves on.

You have the hall fumigated with sulphur.

Then you send Eurycleia upstairs to wake your wife, who has
slept through all of it, because Athena wanted her to.
