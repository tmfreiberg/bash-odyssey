Your son comes up the track at dawn. He is twenty. He has been
at sea for the first time in his life, dodging an ambush he was
warned about by a goddess.

The dogs do not bark at him. That is how Eumaeus knows.

He has never seen your face. He was a baby in arms on a beach
when you sailed. He goes past the old beggar in the doorway with
a nod, because a man of twenty nods at old beggars.

Then Eumaeus goes out to the house with a message for his
mother, so that for one hour there are two of you in that hut.

Athena calls you outside. She takes the disguise off in the
yard. You come back through the door as yourself: taller,
darker, dressed properly, forty-five years old.

He goes white. He backs up. He says: you are a god, stop it, I
have nothing worth taking.

You say: I am not a god. I am your father.

He does not believe it. He argues. No man arrives after twenty
years looking twenty years younger than the beggar who was
sitting there. Then he stops arguing, because you are crying,
because you have started to weep the way a man does when he has
been holding something for two decades, out loud, wretchedly,
without any control over it.

Homer's comparison: birds crying, sea eagles, hook-clawed, when
farmers have taken their young out of the nest before they were
fledged.

Then you put the rags back on.
