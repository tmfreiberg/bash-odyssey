PARNASSUS. The year you turned fifteen.

Your mother's father was Autolycus, who was the best thief of
his generation, who was given that talent by Hermes personally
in exchange for a lifetime of burnt offerings. He named you. He
came to Ithaca when you were born, put you on his knee, said:
I have made enemies of a great many men in my life, so let the
boy be called Odysseus, the man of trouble.

He told your parents to send you to him when you were grown, so
that he could give you presents.

So you went. His sons took you up Parnassus at dawn to hunt.

The boar was lying up in a thicket so dense that no wet wind
blew through it, no sun got in, no rain came through the leaves.
The dogs found him. He came out at you, sideways, fast.

You went in first with the spear held high. He got under it.

He came up along your right leg above the knee, took the flesh
off along the bone with a sideways rip of the tusk, did not
reach the bone. Then your spear went in through his right
shoulder, out the other side, done.

Autolycus' sons bound the wound, sang the charm over it that
stopped the bleeding, carried you down. You were back on Ithaca
before the autumn with a scar you will have for the rest of your
life.

It runs the length of your right thigh, above the knee. It is
white now. It does not tan with the rest of you.

Everybody in your household saw you swimming as a boy.
