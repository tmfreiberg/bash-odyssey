There is a bard in this house called Demodocus. He is blind. The
Muse took his sight, gave him the songs, which is the usual
arrangement.

At dinner they bring him in, seat him, put a lyre in his hands.
He sings what the room asks for. The room asks for Troy.

He sings a quarrel between you and Achilles. He was not there.
He has it right anyway, in detail, the way they do.

You pull your cloak over your head.

You do it under the table edge, with both hands, so that nobody
sees a grown man come apart at his own reputation being sung
back to him by a stranger who has never been within four hundred
miles of the place.

Alcinous notices. He is sitting next to you. He says nothing at
all about it: he calls for the dancing instead, tells everyone
they have had enough of that song, moves the evening on.

Later, after the games, they bring Demodocus back. This time you
ask for a song yourself. You ask for the wooden horse.

He sings it. The hollow horse, the men inside it, the Trojans
arguing on the acropolis about whether to break it open. He
sings the man who thought of it.

The comparison Homer makes for what you do then is not a
comparison anybody expects.

He says: you wept the way a woman weeps, throwing herself across
her husband's body where he has fallen in front of his own city,
holding him while he dies, while the men behind her put spears
into her back to drive her away into slavery.

That is the image. The man who took the city, crying like one of
its widows, at a dinner party, under a cloak, in a country where
nobody has ever been to war.

Alcinous notices again. This time he asks.

He says: enough. Tell us your name. Nobody is nameless. Tell us
where you are from, so that our ships know where to take you.
