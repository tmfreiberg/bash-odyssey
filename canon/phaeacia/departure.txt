They put to sea at dusk. Fifty-two young men on the benches,
picked for it, pulling in time.

Nobody sits at the steering oar. Phaeacian ships do not need
one: they are told the destination once, they find it
themselves, through fog, through night, without a mark on the
water. Alcinous mentions this the way another man would mention
the weather.

They make you a bed on the deck: rug, linen sheet, the stern
where it is quiet.

You are asleep before the oars are wet.

Not dozing. Homer is specific: a sleep like death, sweetest,
deepest, the closest thing to dying without dying. Twenty years
of not sleeping properly ends on a stranger's deck, fifty-two
oars going in the dark above your head, unconscious for the
whole crossing.

You do not see Ithaca appear. You do not see the coast come up.
The one moment you have wanted since you were twenty-five years
old, standing on a beach with a baby in your arms, happens while
you are asleep. You miss it.

They row into a bay at dawn. They lift you off, still asleep,
still wrapped in the linen, put you down on the sand above the
tideline. They carry the gifts up the beach, stack them beside
an olive tree at the head of a cave, well back from anything
passing. Then they go home.

You wake up alone on a beach with treasure piled beside you.

You do not know where you are.

Athena has put a mist over the whole harbour, so that the place
you have been rowing towards for ten years looks like nowhere
you have ever been. The paths look wrong. The headland looks
wrong. You stand up in the sand next to a pile of gold given to
you by people whose island you will never find again, in a
country you cannot identify, alone.

You assume they have cheated you. You count the tripods to check
nothing is missing. Nothing is.
