She is the king's daughter. Her name is Nausicaa. She has come
down to the river mouth with the household laundry in a mule
cart, on a pretext, because a goddess put the idea into her
sleep.

They wash the clothes. They lay them out on the shingle. They
eat, then they play ball on the sand, which is what wakes you.

You come out of the olive scrub with a branch held in front of
you. You have not been dressed in two days. You are crusted with
brine, cut, swollen, entirely wild-looking.

Every one of her women runs.

She does not.

That is the whole of it. That is what she is in this poem for.
A naked stranger comes out of the bushes on an empty beach a
long way from her father's house, her friends scatter, she stays
where she is because she has decided that a person in this state
is owed something.

You do not touch her knees, which is what a suppliant does,
because you work out in the moment that a filthy man taking hold
of a girl's knees on a beach would be the wrong thing entirely.
You stand off. You speak from where you are.

She hears you out. Then she calls her women back, tells them
plainly that this man arrives under the protection of Zeus, has
them give you oil, clothes, food, water.

She tells you the way to the town. She tells you to walk behind
the cart rather than beside it, to give her a head start, to go
to her mother first when you get to the palace, because in this
house the queen is the one who decides.

The best advice anybody gives you in twenty years comes from a
girl doing the washing.
