Patterns -- what grep is really matching

grep does not look for a word. It looks for a pattern. A plain
word is the simplest pattern there is, which is why it works,
but there is a language underneath it.

    ^       the start of a line
    $       the end of a line
    .       any single character
    [abc]   one character out of that set
    [^abc]  one character not out of that set
    *       the thing before this, repeated any number of times

So:

    grep "^We know" song.txt

Every line that BEGINS with those words. A line with "we know"
somewhere in the middle of it does not match.

    grep "sea$" song.txt

Every line ending in sea.

Anchors are what make -v useful. On its own, -v gives you every
line without a word in it. Joined to ^, it gives you every line
that does not begin a certain way, which on a file with a
refrain in it is the whole difference between noise, meaning:

    grep -v "^We know" song.txt

Two more flags worth having. -n puts the line number in front of
each result, so you know where in a file a thing sits:

    grep -n Ithaca song.txt

And -E turns on the fuller pattern language, which includes
alternation. The vertical bar means "or":

    grep -E "Penelope|Telemachus" song.txt

That is one search for two words. Without -E the bar is just a
bar, matched literally, finding nothing.

Quote your patterns. Always. The shell reads several of those
characters as instructions before grep ever sees them.
